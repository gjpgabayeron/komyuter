## REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

This chapter discusses the related literature and studies that served as a guide for the researchers in developing Komyuter.

### PUBLIC TRANSPORTATION IN THE PHILIPPINES

Public transportation in the Philippines faces severe structural challenges that affect millions of commuters daily. According to a study by the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA), traffic congestion in the National Capital Region alone is estimated to cost the Philippine economy at least PhP 3.5 billion daily, totaling a substantial PhP 1.27 trillion annually [9], [22]. The SafeTravelPH Mobility Innovations Organization further noted that 80% of the country's daily trips in Philippine urban regions are taken through public transport such as jeepneys, buses, and UV Express, yet the national budget remains skewed toward road-widening and car-centric projects [37]. The same report noted that only 7.05% of Filipino households own a private car, and these are mostly middle to high-income households, confirming that the majority of Filipinos are dependent on public transportation for their daily travel. Despite this, the total public transport modernization budget for 2026 amounted to only PhP 1.232 billion, which translates to approximately PhP 0.13 per passenger trip per year based on an estimated 20.8 billion public transport passenger trips annually [37].

The absence of digital transit data contributes to these existing problems, particularly in cities outside Metro Manila. In Iloilo City, the Land Transportation Franchising and Regulatory Board (LTFRB) officially turned over the approved enhanced Local Public Transport Route Plan (LPTRP) to the city government in March 2023. Under the original LPTRP, a total of 1,767 modernized jeepneys were authorized to ply the 25 approved routes [11], [35]. However, this route plan exists primarily in paper-based or PDF records, making it inaccessible for use in digital navigation applications. A study by Chang et al., published through the Philippine Institute for Development Studies (PIDS), found that even if the entire PhP 2 trillion public transport infrastructure flagship project pipeline were completed on time over the next decade, the Greater Capital Region would still experience a system-wide shortage in public transport supply [20]. These conditions establish the foundational need for a navigation system such as Komyuter that can make existing route information accessible to commuters.

### SHORTEST PATH ALGORITHMS

The problem of finding the optimal path between two nodes in a graph is foundational to computer science. Dijkstra [5] introduced an algorithm that solves this problem by starting from a source node and iteratively selecting the unvisited node with the lowest accumulated cost, then updating the cost estimates of its neighboring nodes. This process continues until the destination is reached. The algorithm is widely cited for its suitability in routing problems involving large, sparse graphs [3]. Subsequent developments introduced the A* algorithm by Hart, Nilsson, and Raphael [26], which improves search efficiency by incorporating a heuristic estimate of the remaining distance to the destination. More recently, Delling, Pajor, and Werneck [23] proposed the Round-Based Public Transit Routing (RAPTOR) algorithm, which is designed for schedule-based transit systems and optimizes across criteria such as arrival time and number of transfers.

For transit systems where commuters have varying priorities, multi-criteria extensions of Dijkstra's algorithm are applicable. These extensions combine multiple cost factors such as distance, fare, transfer count, and walking distance into a single composite cost value using a weighted linear combination. As long as all weights and component values are non-negative, the algorithm's optimality guarantee is preserved [3]. Komyuter applies this approach through the formula Cost(e) = a x norm(distance) + b x norm(fare) + c x norm(transfer) + d x norm(walk), where users can choose from preference profiles such as Shortest, Cheapest, or Balanced to adjust how each factor is weighted in the route computation.

### GRAPH MODELING OF TRANSIT NETWORKS

Representing transit networks as mathematical graphs is a well-established approach that enables routing algorithms to operate on transit data. In this model, stops are represented as nodes and the service connections between consecutive stops are represented as directed, weighted edges. One of the primary challenges in this approach is handling route transfers, where a passenger alights from one service and boards another. Bast et al. [3] described the route-expanded graph as a widely adopted solution to this problem, wherein each physical stop is assigned a distinct node for every route that passes through it. This allows transfer costs to be explicitly assigned to edges that connect nodes from different routes at the same location. Komyuter follows this convention by assigning a distinct node to each stop on each direction of each route, naming each node as stop_{stopId}_direction_{directionId}, and connecting stop nodes that fall within a 300-meter walking threshold with transfer edges.

Spatial databases such as PostGIS support geographic data types and spatial query functions like ST_ClosestPoint and ST_DWithin, which enable efficient computation of boarding point proximity and polyline projection [33]. Goodchild [6], [25] observed that citizens acting as sensors can generate useful spatial data such as GPS traces that can populate transit graphs where no authoritative digital record exists, a condition directly applicable to Iloilo City. With regard to data accuracy, Merry and Bettinger [13], [32] found that smartphones in urban environments typically exhibit a positional error of 5 to 15 meters, which informed Komyuter's use of a 25 to 50 meter snapping tolerance when projecting passenger locations onto route polylines.

### AUGMENTED REALITY IN NAVIGATION

Augmented reality (AR) navigation overlays digital directional information onto a live camera feed of the user's physical surroundings, providing spatial guidance that complements traditional map-based interfaces. Rehman and Cao [36] found that AR-assisted pedestrian navigation reduced navigation errors and route completion time compared to conventional map interfaces, particularly in dense urban environments where matching map directions to physical surroundings is difficult. Carmigniani et al. [19] further identified public transportation as a high-value context for AR, noting that the difficulty of physically locating an unmarked boarding point from digital directions represents a spatial problem that AR is well positioned to address. There are several types of AR approaches used in navigation, each with distinct characteristics, advantages, and limitations.

Location-Based Geo-AR is a type of AR that computes the screen position of virtual markers using the device's GPS position, compass heading, and device orientation sensors to project points of interest into the user's field of view. This approach does not require physical markers or environmental mapping, making it applicable in outdoor settings without any pre-installed infrastructure [38]. Its primary advantage is deployability — it can function in any outdoor location as long as the device has GPS, an accelerometer, and a magnetometer [38]. However, a documented limitation is sensor instability caused by magnetic interference from urban structures, which can introduce heading errors and displace AR markers at greater distances [38]. Studies have also noted that common outdoor Location-Based AR applications relying on traditional smartphone sensor fusion, such as GPS and compass, often lack the accuracy needed for precise AR content alignment [21]. Despite these limitations, Location-Based Geo-AR remains the most suitable approach for outdoor transit navigation contexts where no fixed infrastructure exists. Komyuter adopts this approach, deriving the bearing to a target stop using the haversine geodetic formula and scaling the AR marker position on screen proportionally to the estimated distance.

Vision-Based Marker AR is another approach wherein the system recognizes predefined visual patterns, commonly known as fiducial markers, through the device camera and uses them as reference points to overlay digital content. This method provides fast, accurate, and real-time tracking and is widely used in indoor AR applications [19]. Unlike Location-Based Geo-AR, marker-based AR does not rely on GPS and is therefore not subject to GPS drift or compass interference. However, its major limitation is the requirement for physical marker installation in the environment, which makes it impractical for outdoor or large-scale transit deployments where stops are unmarked or distributed across wide geographic areas. Research has noted that marker-based tracking is rarely used for large outdoor AR applications precisely because of this constraint [38]. For Komyuter, this approach was not adopted because jeepney boarding points in Iloilo City are not fixed physical locations and do not have installed infrastructure.

Geofencing with event triggers is a complementary technology that defines virtual geographic boundaries around real-world locations. When a user's device enters or exits a defined boundary, a predefined action is triggered in the application [24]. In the context of AR navigation, geofencing can be used to activate or deactivate AR overlays based on the user's proximity to a point of interest, such as automatically switching from map view to AR view when the user is within a defined radius of a jeepney stop. This approach has been applied in indoor AR navigation frameworks to manage large coordinate spaces and reduce error accumulation in camera-based tracking [29]. For Komyuter, geofencing is considered as a future improvement to the existing Location-Based Geo-AR implementation, with the goal of creating a hybrid system that triggers AR mode automatically when the user enters the proximity zone of a target stop, reducing the need for manual mode switching and improving the overall navigation experience.

### USABILITY EVALUATION METHODS

Usability evaluation in software systems research is commonly conducted through standardized post-study questionnaires administered after structured task scenarios, which allow comparison against established benchmarks [31]. The Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire (PSSUQ), developed by Lewis [12], [30] at IBM, consists of 16 items organized into three subscales: System Usefulness (Items 1 to 6), Information Quality (Items 7 to 12), and Interface Quality (Items 13 to 16). Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale where lower scores indicate greater satisfaction. Lewis [12], [30] reported a Cronbach's alpha above 0.90 across the subscales, indicating high internal consistency. A subsequent study by Lewis [31] established an industry benchmark mean of 2.82 for the overall PSSUQ scale, which serves as a reference point for interpreting study results. The PSSUQ was selected for use in this study over the System Usability Scale (SUS) because it provides separate subscale scores, enabling the researchers to distinguish the usability of the navigation component from that of the AR wayfinding component.

### RELATED SYSTEMS AND STUDIES

Several existing navigation systems are relevant to Komyuter. Google Maps Transit does not provide public transit directions for Iloilo City due to the absence of machine-readable General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS) data for the city [16]. Sakay.ph is a transit navigation application built for the Philippine context, offering route recommendations for jeepney, bus, and MRT connections in Metro Manila based on manually curated route data [2]. However, its coverage is limited to Metro Manila, and it does not include AR wayfinding or user-adjustable multi-criteria routing. Moovit operates in select Philippine cities using agency-supplied data but provides incomplete route coverage for Iloilo City and fare estimates that do not follow the LTFRB distance-based formula [28].

A convergent research initiative from a separate Philippine institution independently developed a jeepney navigation prototype for Iloilo City using a comparable algorithmic and geographic scope, providing external validation that the gap in transit navigation for the city is recognized within the research community. The methodological details of that work differ from Komyuter's architecture, particularly in the absence of AR wayfinding and LTFRB-compliant fare computation [27]. Research from comparable transit contexts in developing cities has also shown that static route-based navigation provides substantial value to commuters even in the absence of real-time vehicle tracking data [34], which supports the approach used in Komyuter.

### SYNTHESIS

The literature reviewed across the six themes collectively supports the development of Komyuter. The SafeTravelPH [37] and PIDS [20] documents confirm that the public transport shortage in Philippine cities, including Iloilo, is structural and not adequately addressed by existing infrastructure investments alone. The PNA [11], [35] report confirms that Iloilo City's route data exists under the LPTRP but remains in a format that cannot be processed by navigation applications, establishing the need for a system that can operationalize this data. The literature on shortest path algorithms supports the use of a multi-criteria extension of Dijkstra's algorithm as a theoretically sound method for route optimization that reflects the varying priorities of jeepney commuters [3], [5]. The route-expanded graph model provides a structured approach to representing PUJ corridors and transfer points [3], while findings on GPS accuracy [13], [32] and volunteered geographic information [6], [25] support the spatial modeling decisions made in the system. The AR navigation literature confirms the utility of location-based AR in addressing the last-meter boarding problem [19], and the PSSUQ is validated as an appropriate instrument for evaluating the usability of the system across its functional components [12], [30], [31]. A review of related systems confirms that no existing tool integrates multi-criteria Dijkstra pathfinding, LTFRB-compliant fare computation, and AR-assisted boarding guidance into a single mobile application for Iloilo City commuters, establishing a clear gap that this study addresses.
